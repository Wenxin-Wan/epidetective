What causes cancer and other chronic diseases, and how sure the evidence is

A personal project by Wenxin Wan, a cancer epidemiologist. Free tools built from the IARC Monographs and from published reviews. They describe whole populations; they cannot tell you what caused any one person’s illness.

For the curious public, clinicians, students and journalists.

Four questions, answered from the tools

  1. Is aspartame dangerous?

    In June 2023 IARC classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B), a rating of hazard. WHO says a 70-kilogram adult would need more than 9 to 14 cans of diet drink a day to pass [the acceptable daily intake].

    Quoted from the Cancer Evidence Explorer

    Open the Aspartame entry →

  2. Is processed meat as bad as smoking?

    Same certainty (Group 1), very different size.

    Each 50 grams eaten every day raises the risk of bowel cancer by 18%. In European data, about 10 to 12 in 100 smokers get lung cancer by age 75, compared with 4 to 5 in 1,000 lifelong non-smokers.

    Quoted from the Cancer Evidence Explorer

    Open the Processed meat entry → Relative and absolute risk →

  3. Does my job raise my cancer risk?

    68 workplace agents and jobs are on the cancer map, from asbestos and silica dust to night-shift work and firefighting.

    Painters with over 10 years in the trade had about 1.8 times the bladder-cancer risk of non-painters. Lung-cancer risk rises with the total silica breathed over the years.

    Quoted from the Cancer Evidence Explorer

    Work and cancer, sector by sector →

  4. What raises stroke risk?

    Twelve risk factors are graded for ischaemic stroke, from blood pressure, smoking and diabetes (Strong) to depression (Suggestive).

    Blood pressure is the single largest modifiable contributor to stroke worldwide.

    Quoted from the Chronic Disease Evidence Explorer

    Open the Ischaemic stroke entry →

Cancer Evidence Explorer

See which exposures have been linked to which cancers, and how strong the evidence is behind each link. Its data come from the IARC Monographs, in which independent Working Groups convened by IARC evaluate what can cause cancer. Start from a cancer, start from an exposure, or open the whole map. Counted here: every agent for which IARC’s cancer-site table lists at least limited evidence in people for a named cancer (Volumes 1 to 141), adapted as the tool describes.

EpiDetective publishes tools for reading evidence about whole populations. They show patterns across large groups of people. For anything concerning your own health, speak to a doctor. Each tool lists its own sources.
EpiDetective is a personal project by Wenxin Wan, produced in a personal capacity. It carries no endorsement from the International Agency for Research on Cancer or the World Health Organization, and the views expressed here belong to the author alone. The underlying scientific sources are cited so that anyone can check them independently.
