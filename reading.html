EpiDetective · Reading the evidence

Seven ideas that make health evidence readable.

Every number in the tools on this site rests on a few ideas from epidemiology. None of them needs mathematics. Read them once and a health headline becomes much easier to weigh.

Hazard and risk

A hazard is something that can cause harm. Risk is how likely the harm is.

Sunlight, alcohol and asbestos are all hazards: each can cause cancer. How likely that is for any one person depends on how much, for how long and at what age. The IARC Monographs identify hazards and leave the size of the risk to other studies.

Relative and absolute risk

“Twice the risk” means little until you know twice what.

A relative risk compares two groups. An absolute risk says how many people are affected. Doubling a risk of 1 in 10,000 adds one case among 10,000 people; a 10% rise in a risk of 1 in 10 adds a hundred among the same 10,000. Ask for both numbers.

Association and cause

Two things can rise together without one causing the other.

People who carry a lighter develop more lung cancer, and the lighter is innocent: smoking explains both. Epidemiologists call this confounding. Before they call a link causal, they look for the same result across many studies, for more disease where there is more exposure, and for the exposure coming first.

See the evidence Tobacco smoking

Chance and bias

A result can come from luck, or lean one way because of how the study was run.

Chance. Every study looks at a sample of people, and a small sample can show a link, or hide one, by luck alone. Toss a fair coin ten times and you get seven heads or more about one time in six; toss it a thousand times and the share of heads stays close to half. That is why one small study rarely settles a question.

Bias. A study can lean one way because of how it was run. The Interphone study asked people to recall their past mobile phone use, and heavy users tended to overestimate theirs. IARC said chance or bias may explain the higher risk of glioma, a brain tumour, seen in the heaviest users.

See the evidence Radiofrequency fields (e.g. mobile phones)

The range around a number

Every estimate comes with a range, and the range is part of the result.

A study might report a relative risk of 1.2 with a 95% confidence interval from 0.9 to 1.6. The data fit a small increase, and they fit no effect at all just as well, because the interval includes 1. A narrow interval from a large study deserves more weight than a wide one from a small study.

See the evidence Unprocessed red meat and coronary heart disease

No evidence, and evidence of no effect

“We could not tell” and “it is safe” are two different findings.

When IARC places an agent in Group 3, “not classifiable”, the studies were usually too few or too weak to decide either way. That describes the state of the evidence. Group 3 is also used when strong evidence shows that the way an agent causes cancer in animals does not operate in people, and nothing else supports a higher group. Since the 2019 Preamble, IARC no longer has a Group 4 (“probably not carcinogenic”), and agents that would once have gone there now go in Group 3. Showing that something is safe takes large, careful studies that looked for harm and found none.

Populations and people

These numbers describe groups. They cannot explain one person’s illness.

A risk factor shifts the odds across thousands of people. Most people with a given exposure never develop the disease, and some people without it do. For anything about your own health, ask the people treating you.

Worked example

One headline, read with the seven ideas.

“Processed meat causes cancer, says WHO”

Headlines like this one followed IARC’s evaluation of red and processed meat in October 2015. Here is the same news, read with each idea in turn.

  1. Hazard and risk

    IARC placed processed meat in Group 1, its most certain group, because the evidence in people is sufficient for bowel cancer. That answers whether it can cause cancer. How much it raises the risk is a separate question.

  2. Relative and absolute risk

    The tool’s entry says: “Each 50 grams eaten every day raises the risk of bowel cancer by 18%.” That is a relative risk. Tobacco smoking is in Group 1 too, and its entry gives absolute numbers: “In European data, about 10 to 12 in 100 smokers get lung cancer by age 75, compared with 4 to 5 in 1,000 lifelong non-smokers.” Same certainty, very different size.

  3. Association and cause

    People who eat a lot of processed meat may also differ in other ways, for example in how much they smoke or exercise. A verdict of sufficient evidence means IARC’s Working Group judged, with reasonable confidence, that such differences do not explain the link.

  4. Chance and bias

    The same verdict means chance and bias were ruled out with reasonable confidence too. That judgement weighs the whole body of studies. A single small study could not carry it.

  5. The range around a number

    The 18% is a central estimate from pooled studies, and like every estimate it comes with a range. Ask for the range before you weigh the number: if it includes no effect, the data also fit no rise at all.

  6. No evidence, and evidence of no effect

    IARC’s verdict covers processed meat and, in Group 2A, red meat. Other foods were outside this evaluation, so the headline says nothing about whether they are safe.

  7. Populations and people

    These figures describe large groups. They cannot tell anyone whether a particular bowel cancer came from eating ham. Most people who eat processed meat never get bowel cancer, and some who never eat it do.

See the evidence Processed meat Tobacco smoking

A hazard says whether something can cause harm. A hazard ratio is a study’s number comparing how fast harm happens in two groups. The words look alike and mean different things.

Go further

Read further, and test yourself.

  • The IARC Monographs Preamble (amended January 2019): the rules IARC follows to weigh the evidence and assign its groups.
  • Causal Inference: What If, by Miguel A. Hernán and James M. Robins (2020): a free textbook on telling cause from association.

Five questions to test yourself

Think of an answer first, then open the question to compare.

Sunlight and processed meat are both in IARC’s Group 1. Are they equally dangerous?

No. Group 1 says the evidence that each can cause cancer is equally sure. How much each one raises the risk is a separate question, and the answers differ widely.

A headline says a food “doubles the risk” of a rare cancer. What do you need to know before you worry?

How common the cancer is to begin with. Doubling a risk of 1 in 10,000 adds one case among 10,000 people.

Towns that sell more ice cream have more drownings. Does ice cream cause drowning?

No. Hot weather brings both: people eat more ice cream and swim more. A third factor that drives two things at once is called a confounder.

A study reports a relative risk of 1.3, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.8 to 2.1. Has it shown that the risk goes up?

No. The interval includes 1, so the data also fit no change in risk. A larger study would narrow the range.

IARC places an agent in Group 3, “not classifiable”. Can you say it is safe?

No. Group 3 means the evidence was too limited to decide. Showing that something is safe takes large studies that looked for harm and found none.
