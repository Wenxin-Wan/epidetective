## A laugh in the seminar room

Some years ago, I sat in a seminar where a PhD student presented her work on coffee and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Her results were mostly null: coffee drinkers had no higher risk of COPD. When the last slide came up, the room relaxed. People lifted their mugs, looked at one another and laughed.

Nobody was laughing at her. She had done exactly what PhD students are trained to do. Still, the moment stayed with me. It showed how far the question, and the motive behind it, had drifted from anything people outside that room would call a problem.

I have thought about that laugh often since, especially in the past few years, as epidemiology and artificial intelligence both stopped needing an introduction. The pandemic taught the world about reproduction numbers and flattened curves. ChatGPT, released in November 2022, made large language models an everyday tool. They generate text by repeatedly predicting what comes next, drawing on patterns learned from enormous amounts of writing, a process made practical by vast computing power.

## What is epidemiology for?

Much of epidemiology falls into two broad branches. One gathers data on populations and uses study designs to answer causal questions: does X cause Y? The other models how often a disease, usually an infectious one, occurs in a population and how fast it spreads.

Both draw on a shared habit of mind. We look at a pattern and ask what might explain it, keeping in mind the things that could fool us: confounding, reverse causation, selection bias and the rest. None of this belongs to us alone. Economists, sociologists and psychologists also work with observational data and worry about many of the same traps, although with slightly different terminology.

That raises an awkward question, one I suspect many epidemiologists ask privately but rarely out loud. How does our field change the way we understand the world? Physics gives us the laws of the universe. Chemistry gives us the elements that make up our world. We, on the other hand, give relative risks with confidence intervals attached, and conclusions so heavily hedged that they are correct but tell people little about their own health risk.

## The glut

Each year, that question gets harder to answer because each year we publish more. Large public resources such as UK Biobank and the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) are analysed again and again, often to ask whether coffee or tea changes the risk of brain cancer. In my view, such studies give the public little, and sometimes less than nothing: each adds another contradictory headline to the pile.

The complaint is familiar. The numbers behind it still make me wince. In a 2013 paper, Jonathan Schoenfeld and John Ioannidis [selected 50 ingredients from randomly chosen recipes in an old American cookbook](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23193004/) and searched the literature for each. Forty had been studied in relation to cancer. Among these, most individual studies claimed an effect in one direction or the other, but three-quarters of the estimates offered weak statistical evidence or fell short of the usual significance threshold, and the effects shrank when pooled in meta-analyses. More recently, a group at the University of Surrey [counted formulaic NHANES papers](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3003152) linking a single predictor to a single disease. They found about four a year from 2014 to 2021, followed by 33 in 2022, 82 in 2023 and 190 in 2024 up to early October. The authors point to AI-ready data and AI-assisted workflows, warning that the formula is easy for paper mills to exploit.

Inside the ivory tower, the pressure to publish falls on everyone. Young investigators need papers to survive; senior ones need them to keep their groups funded. So some of us, at some point, have published work we privately doubted. The pressure is most painful for young researchers, who seldom choose what they work on: most inherit projects built on proposals their supervisors wrote to impress a funder, with promises too ambitious for the time and money available.

## Shoe leather

Ask most of us what makes a good question, and we give the stock answer: it should come from the real world, address a real problem and point towards prevention. Everyone nods. Then the millions go to chasing chance findings.

My answer is the old-fashioned one. Epidemiologists should not stay indoors all day. The people who built this field wore out their shoes: they collected data first-hand, interviewed the sick and took their own measurements. Looking back at John Snow’s work on cholera in 1850s London, the statistician David Freedman credited its strength to clear reasoning and sheer legwork, far more than to statistical technique. He called that legwork [shoe leather](https://www.jstor.org/stable/270939).

Today, most of us sit at a computer studying numbers someone else gathered through a questionnaire. We hope the people who filled it in answered carefully, rather than rushing to finish and move on to something more engaging. Survey researchers have a name for this: [satisficing](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2350050305), or settling for an answer that seems good enough. It is a familiar limitation, but familiarity should not make it easier to ignore.

Epidemiology should serve one purpose above all: stopping people from getting sick. The starting point is just as plain. Count the sick. Look for the anomaly. Then investigate its cause, using a carefully chosen reference group.

Two old cases show how far that can take us. Between 1966 and 1969, doctors in Boston treated eight young women in their teens and early twenties for adenocarcinoma of the vagina, a cancer almost unheard of at that age. Arthur Herbst and his colleagues matched each patient with four controls and interviewed their mothers. [Seven of the eight patients’ mothers](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197104222841604) had taken diethylstilbestrol, a synthetic oestrogen, early in pregnancy. None of the 32 control mothers had.

A few years later, John Creech, the plant physician at a vinyl chloride factory near Louisville, Kentucky, and Maurice Johnson, the company’s medical director, took careful histories from men with angiosarcoma of the liver. The tumour was so rare that the whole United States saw perhaps 25 cases a year. They realised the men had all worked in [the same polymerisation section](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00046136.htm). In 1974, US regulators proposed cutting the exposure limit 500-fold, from 500 parts per million to one. Industry said it could not be done; within a year, a closed-loop process made it routine. Among workers exposed only after the change, the tumour has all but vanished.

Neither discovery needed a biobank or a machine-learning pipeline. Both needed someone close enough to the patients to notice that something was wrong, and curious enough to ask the next question. These were rare tumours with huge relative risks, about the loudest signals our field ever gets. Most of what we study now is far quieter. That makes the habit of looking closely at real patients all the more valuable. Chasing down a cause is the most interesting thing we do. Complex algorithms and statistical jargon should not bury it.

## What AI changes

In the near term, the first thing AI changes is the cost of routine work. Screening literature, writing code, drafting text: machines can already speed up all of it. On this, I agree with most commentators, including [David Bann and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyaf210) at University College London, writing in the *International Journal of Epidemiology* this year. In their tests, frontier models wrote complete epidemiology papers from a plain-language prompt and a simulated dataset. Like them, I expect that much of the routine labour in our field will simply disappear.

Their sharper warning concerns who loses. Junior epidemiologists have traditionally done the analysis and writing under a senior colleague’s supervision. If that work goes, so does much of the apprenticeship that trains the next generation. Left unchecked, the authors suggest, this could lead to the discipline’s collapse. The wider labour market already shows a similar pattern. Using US payroll data, a Stanford team found that by mid-2026, [employment among 22- to 25-year-olds](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Canaries_BrynjolfssonChandarChen.pdf) in the most AI-exposed jobs was about a fifth below where it would have been had it kept pace with less exposed jobs. By contrast, employment among older workers in the same jobs continued to grow. Erik Brynjolfsson and his co-authors read the gap as an early warning, a canary in the coal mine, though they stop short of claiming that AI caused it.

The second change brings us back to volume. Output was already outrunning review capacity before ChatGPT: articles indexed in Scopus and Web of Science [increased by about 47% between 2016 and 2022](https://doi.org/10.1162/qss_a_00327), far faster than the number of working scientists. The NHANES figures above suggest what happens as the cost of producing a paper falls towards zero. Editors face a flood of polished manuscripts, each with a confident paragraph explaining why its findings matter, and a shrinking pool of willing reviewers. Who has time to check the numbers when, as I suspect, the average quality of submissions keeps slipping?

Mathematics has just shown us what that flood looks like. On 6 October, OpenAI posted [722 AI-written manuscripts](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/08/openai-withdraws-preprints-722-manuscripts-unsolved-math-problems/) claiming progress on 372 open problems, an episode since dubbed the “mathocalypse”. Within a day, three were withdrawn because of a sign error and 14 more were revised; about half the results had not been formally verified when they went up. Yet some of the work holds: number theorists who have examined one headline result, a weak form of the Riemann hypothesis, [largely agree that it is correct](https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/). That mix is what makes it hard. Because some results may be right, none can be dismissed out of hand, so every one of the 722 still needs a human expert to check it and understand it.

## What is left for us

On balance, I am pessimistic, both about the field and about the young, eager epidemiologists expected to carry it forward. I suspect other empirical, data-hungry disciplines face much the same prospect. Mathematics suggests that even deductive fields are not safe.

There is one hopeful note, and it comes from how people think. Our instincts, creativity and value judgements grow out of reasoning tangled up with personal experience and, yes, personal bias. That mixture allows us to produce ideas a reasoning machine might dismiss as illogical. Most of those ideas may be wrong, but a few can move whole fields.

In epidemiology, this might mean developing a more humane understanding of exposure: why people knowingly put themselves in harm’s way, through their work or their habits. It might mean treating information itself as an exposure, studying how a view spreads through a population as we once traced a virus. Public health has already started down this road. Heidi Larson [warned in 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-018-07034-4) that the next major outbreak might be driven more by emotional contagion eroding trust in vaccines than by a shortage of vaccines themselves. Two years later, the WHO was running a programme to address the COVID-19 [“infodemic”](https://www.who.int/health-topics/infodemic).

Both ideas take us beyond epidemiology, and I think that is exactly what we need. Our field draws heavily on biology and psychology. We study how disease is distributed across populations, but often forget that populations are made of real people. To explain those patterns, we need to understand how people think and how living systems respond to changing environments. To understand why whole populations live so differently, we need economists and sociologists too. A relative risk might tell me that workers who leave their respirators on the hook fall ill more often. It cannot tell me why they leave them there.

So we should not fence ourselves in. The answer to a question like that often sits over the fence, in someone else’s journal. We should stop letting vocabulary decide where one field ends and the next begins. We should cultivate the things we still do better than machines: create and combine concepts, reason across them, and judge which questions deserve our time. Each gives us another way to approach the question that has always mattered most in our field.

**Why are some people sick?**

Geoffrey Rose, in a [lecture later published in 1985](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/14.1.32), split that question in two: why some individuals fall ill, and why some populations carry so much more illness than others. A model trained on a biobank may soon predict, better than any of us, which individuals will fall ill. The second question still needs someone prepared to wear out their shoes.
