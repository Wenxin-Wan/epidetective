02

Carcinogens at work, by sector and region

Silica, asbestos, diesel exhaust and wood dust are met mostly at work, and outdoor workers spend long hours in the sun. Global models estimate exposure for most countries; surveys measure it sector by sector in only a few. How many people work in each sector, known for most countries, hints at where these exposures could matter most.

Where exposure fell, and where it persists

Bans, laws, vaccines and new fuels have cut some exposures sharply in some countries, while other exposures persist. Open a card for the figures and their sources.

04

Known exposures without a country map

For these agents no source counts exposure country by country. What is known comes from regional studies, outbreaks or surveys of one place. Group 1 means IARC classifies the agent as a cause of cancer in humans; Group 2A, as a probable cause.

Each map layer comes from one published source, named on the layer with its year and terms of use. Figures taken from papers and reports were saved with their source files, and a script checked that every figure and its quotation appear in those files; figures read from images were checked by a second reader.

The atlas estimates nothing for a country its source leaves blank. Where a country has no figure for the year chosen, its latest earlier figure is shown, marked with its own year, if it is recent enough (within 1 to 10 years, depending on the layer). Regional panels show the median and range of the countries in each region. The only step the atlas models itself is the illustration in the work section, which applies the six EU countries' sector rates to each region's workforce and says so.

How common an exposure is says little on its own about how many cancers it causes. A country where many people are exposed to a weak cause can have fewer cancers than a country where fewer people are exposed to a strong one, and the amount and duration of exposure matter too. The Cancer Evidence Explorer gives the evidence that each agent causes cancer.

Modelled: an estimate by WHO, UNICEF, ILO, UNAIDS or the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study that combines surveys and reported data with statistical models.

Reported: measured in national surveys or compiled from national statistics, as published.

Study-based: pooled from published studies that differ in design and year.

Policy: a law or programme that limits exposure.

Summary exposure value (SEV): the GBD study's measure, the share of people exposed weighted by how much each level of exposure raises risk, from 0 to 100. For a yes-or-no exposure such as secondhand smoke it equals the share of people exposed.
