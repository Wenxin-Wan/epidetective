Cancer awareness games

Sol & the UV Shield Quest

A side-scrolling adventure that teaches children how to enjoy the sun without harming their skin.

Most skin cancers are caused by ultraviolet radiation, and the habits that prevent them are learned young. In this game Sol runs, jumps and collects sun stars across five regions, from a grassy meadow to a snowy mountain. Along the way Sol picks up a broad-brimmed hat and SPF 30+ sunscreen, loses the sunscreen after a swim, and reaches checkpoints where a quiz question must be answered before the path opens again.

The questions cover what the UV Index means, when protection matters (UV 3 and above), why shade, clothing and sunscreen work best together, how sand, water and snow reflect UV, and why tanning beds should be avoided. Wrong answers are never punished: Sol is nudged back with a hint and a laugh. The game runs in your browser, works on a phone, and collects no data. It is in English.

6–12recommended age
5regions to cross
30questions in the bank, 5 per run
~10 minfor a full run

How to play

  1. Press Play. On a keyboard, move with A D or ← → and jump with Space. On a phone, use the on-screen buttons. A game controller works too.
  2. Collect sun coins and the nine hidden sun stars. Some platforms move, some fall, so keep moving.
  3. Watch the UV Index in the top bar. It changes from region to region, and drops when Sol stands in shade.
  4. At each checkpoint, read the question and jump up beneath one of the three answer blocks. The right answer opens the gate; a wrong one gives a hint and another try.
  5. Press R to restart. The cog opens the settings: voice narration, high contrast, a dyslexia-friendly font, larger text, game speed and touch-control size.

The five regions and their UV Index

  • Sunny Grassland UV 5
  • Shade Forest UV 3
  • Sunscreen Beach UV 7
  • Sports & Mountain UV 8
  • Sun-Safety Castle UV 6

The game uses an original character and artwork. Its source is open at github.com/Wenxin-Wan/sun-safety-adventure. The sun-safety advice follows the European Code Against Cancer and WHO guidance on UV protection.

Investigation game · lung cancer

The Lung Detective

An investigation across a flat, a street and a building yard, in which every clue is filed the way IARC files evidence.

Smoking causes most lung cancers, and stopping helps at whatever age it happens. That is the first finding in the case file, and the rest of the investigation builds on it. The player searches three scenes, a flat, a street and a building yard, and turns up twelve clues. Each has to be filed the way IARC files evidence: a known cause of lung cancer (Group 1), a probable cause (Group 2A), or something with no evidence behind it. The real hazards can then be fixed, and a clean-air score rises with each one.

The clues cover second-hand smoke; radon in the home, with a small detector game built around the WHO reference level of 100 Bq/m³; outdoor air pollution and diesel exhaust; wood smoke and high-temperature frying; and the silica dust, asbestos and welding fumes of a building site. Bitumen roofing lands in the probable group, and coffee and stress turn out to have no evidence behind them at all. Three conversations carry the argument: a flatmate who smokes only a few a day, a vape-shop owner who calls vaping harmless, and a foreman who thinks quitting after twenty years is pointless. Every clue shows its source and the official wording of the European Code Against Cancer. The game runs in your browser, works on a phone, and collects no data. It is in English.

10–16recommended age
3scenes to search
12clues to file
~10 minfor a full run

How to play

  1. Press Play. Use the mouse or a finger, or Tab to reach a clue and Enter to inspect it. The pulsing rings mark where the clues are.
  2. File each clue in one of three drawers: known cause (Group 1), probable cause (Group 2A), or no evidence. A wrong drawer is greyed out and you try again; the right one opens the source and the wording of the European Code Against Cancer.
  3. Fix the hazards that can be fixed. Each one raises the clean-air score. In the flat, the radon detector is a small game of its own: the WHO reference level is 100 Bq/m³, and WHO says national reference levels should not exceed 300.
  4. Talk to the three people you meet: the flatmate, the vape-shop owner and the foreman. Each has a reason to doubt, and each gets the evidence, kindly.
  5. The cog opens the settings: high contrast, larger text, wider and easier-to-read letters, and reduced motion. The report at the end shows what was filed, what was fixed and the clean-air score.

Every clue in the game cites its source. The classifications follow the IARC Monographs, the prevention wording is that of the European Code Against Cancer, and the radon reference level is the WHO's (100 Bq/m³). The finding that stopping smoking helps at any age comes from Peto and colleagues (BMJ, 2000).

Choices game · breast cancer

A Town of 1,000

A choices game in which a town council follows 1,000 women from their twenties to their sixties and sees, dot by dot, what prevention changes.

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women, and much of what is said about preventing it rests on very little. In this game the player is the council of a town whose 1,000 women are followed from their twenties to their sixties. Each of five chapters brings two decision cards and a small effort budget to spend on them: alcohol, physical activity, weight after the menopause, breastfeeding, menopausal hormone therapy, screening. The outcome appears on an array of 1,000 dots as the number of women diagnosed with breast cancer by age 75, about 94 of 1,000 in Western Europe (GLOBOCAN 2024 estimates), and the number who die of it, about 15.

The effect sizes are modest and honest, taken from pooled studies, so a good decade of choices moves a handful of dots. Myth cards are there to be spotted: antiperspirants and underwired bras cost effort and change nothing, and a smoke-free card explains that for breast cancer the evidence on smoking is limited, whatever smoking does elsewhere in the body. The game ends with an honesty panel: these are population averages, and they predict nothing about any one woman. It runs in your browser, works on a phone, and collects no data. It is in English.

14+recommended age
5chapters, one per decade
10decision cards, two per chapter
~8 minfor a full run

How to play

  1. Press Play. You sit as the council of a town whose 1,000 women you will follow across five decades, one chapter for each decade of their lives.
  2. Each chapter deals two decision cards and gives you a small effort budget. Read both cards, then spend the budget where you think it will count.
  3. Watch the 1,000 dots. Each choice moves the number of women diagnosed by age 75, and the number who die of it, by the amount the pooled studies support. Small movements are the honest ones.
  4. Some cards are myths. Antiperspirants and underwired bras cost effort and move nothing; the smoke-free card explains why the breast-cancer evidence for smoking is only limited.
  5. Read the honesty panel at the end. The numbers are averages for a population like Western Europe's, and no dot stands for any one woman.

Baseline figures are for Western Europe (GLOBOCAN 2024 estimates). Effect sizes come from pooled studies of alcohol, physical activity, weight after the menopause, breastfeeding, menopausal hormone therapy and screening participation. The prevention advice follows the European Code Against Cancer.

EpiDetective publishes tools for reading evidence about whole populations. They show patterns across large groups of people. For anything concerning your own health, speak to a doctor. Each tool lists its own sources.
