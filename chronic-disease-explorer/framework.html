1. The problem this project faces that the cancer project did not

The cancer explorer had an enormous advantage. IARC had already done the adjudication. Group 1, 2A, 2B and 3 is a published, expert-consensus, internationally recognised classification, so the site only had to present it.

Chronic disease has no IARC. No single body says "high sodium intake is a cause of stroke" with the authority of a Monograph. What exists instead is:

  • thousands of overlapping meta-analyses of observational cohorts,
  • a well-documented history of confidently held associations that reversed when tested properly,
  • and several grading systems (WCRF/AICR, GRADE, USPSTF, GBD, umbrella-review classes) that disagree with one another.

Any project that maps chronic disease risk factors has to face this directly or it will mislead. This document sets out how this project does that. Where a body does rule on a risk-outcome pair, the project records the ruling and uses it as one line of evidence (section 10.2).

2. Critique of the first score proposal

The first proposal was:

score = (number of systematic reviews showing a positive association) × (strength of that association, i.e. RR or meta-RR)

The idea is sound and the project kept it: evidence strength has a replication dimension and a magnitude dimension, and both should count. The specific formula has six defects that would make the output indefensible at peer review.

2.1 A plain product makes unlike things look alike. Twenty reviews each reporting RR 1.1 score 22. One review reporting RR 20 scores 20. The two situations differ a great deal, yet the scores are almost the same. Count and magnitude have to be combined on a scale where each levels off.

2.2 Counting systematic reviews counts research attention. Diet and cardiovascular disease have hundreds of meta-analyses. Occupational silica and chronic kidney disease have a handful. The difference reflects funding, fashion and ease of study, and it says little about the strength of causation. Uncorrected, the score would rank well-funded fields above well-established ones.

2.3 Systematic reviews on the same question share their studies. Eight meta-analyses of the same relationship usually pool largely the same cohorts. Counting them as eight confirmations counts the same participants eight times. The literature calls this the overlapping-reviews problem and measures it as the corrected covered area. Replication needs sharply diminishing returns and a discount for overlap.

2.4 RRs can be compared only when the exposure contrast is fixed. "RR 1.2" means little alone. Per 1 SD? Per 50 g/day? Highest against lowest quintile? Ever against never? Extreme-quantile contrasts routinely give RRs two to three times larger than per-unit contrasts for the same underlying relationship. Comparing them without normalisation is the commonest error in ranking risk factors across a field.

2.5 A multiplicative score mishandles protective factors. Physical activity and colon cancer has RR ≈ 0.80. Under the raw formula that scores below a null association. Direction has to be separated from magnitude, using |ln(RR)|, so that RR 0.5 and RR 2.0 register as equally strong in opposite directions.

2.6 The decisive objection: the formula has no term for bias. Observational chronic disease epidemiology is where bias lives, and the history of the field shows it. Applying "many reviews × large RR" to the evidence base as it stood at the time would have graded each of these as strong:

AssociationObservational verdictWhat happened

Every one of these would have scored highly on count × RR. Each was overturned by randomised or genetic evidence. A score that ignores triangulation reproduces the field's most famous failures. Confounding by healthy-user behaviour, reverse causation and selection into exposure groups do not average away with more meta-analyses. More meta-analyses of the same biased cohorts make the estimate more precise and no less wrong.

3. The framework: three quantities

Three quantities are reported separately, because they answer different questions: how sure the evidence is of a cause (the grade), how large the effect is (the estimate), and how much replicated evidence supports it (the score).

3.1 Axis 1: the evidence grade

A five-level grade answers one question: is the relationship causal? The levels are harmonised with the established frameworks. The grade is judged for the outcome the estimate measures (section 6).

GradeWeight CWhat it tells the readerClosest outside ratings
Strong1.00Different kinds of evidence agree that this is a cause, and a reversal is unlikely.Burden of Proof 4 or 5 stars; umbrella Class I; GRADE high
Moderate0.80Probably a cause: studies agree and show a gradient or a known mechanism, but one strand of proof is still missing.3 stars; umbrella Class II
Suggestive0.55A consistent association, but confounding or reverse causation has not been ruled out.2 stars; umbrella Class III
Weak0.30A signal from few, inconsistent or bias-prone studies. It may not hold up.1 star; umbrella Class IV
Conflicting0.25Good evidence points in opposite directions, often observational studies against trials or genetic studies.none

The one-line definitions in the third column appear on every panel of the page. None of the five names is an IARC evidence category.

Versions before 2.0 used the names Convincing, Probable, Suggestive, Limited and Contested. Convincing became Strong, Probable became Moderate, Limited became Weak and Contested became Conflicting. Suggestive keeps its name. The weights are unchanged.

The Conflicting grade is deliberate. Standard frameworks have no good home for "the cohorts say one thing and the trials say another". That situation is common, and it is the one readers most need flagged. Vitamin D deficiency and coronary heart disease sits here: cohort studies show a link, and the large supplementation trials and Mendelian randomisation find none. Arsenic in drinking water and type 2 diabetes is a second kind. Syntheses disagree with one another: studies in places with highly contaminated water find a clear association, and a later analysis restricted to the range that drinking-water standards cover finds none.

3.2 How a grade is set: the decision table

Version 1.0 left grade assignment to expert judgement and recorded the inputs. Version 2.0 states the rules. A script applies them to the recorded fields of every link, and a curator confirms or overrides the result.

3.2.1 How the table works

  • Each rule reads recorded fields. A rule that fails sets a ceiling, the highest grade the link can hold. The table grade is the lowest ceiling.
  • Conflicting is tested first (rules C1 to C4).
  • The table lowers grades and never raises them. A link where the table finds no recorded reason for its Weak or Suggestive grade becomes "cannot tell" and goes to a curator. Where a field is not yet recorded, the script reads the text of caveat, review_note, mediation and quote as a proxy.
  • Each rule carries a confidence. A change that rests only on low-confidence rules becomes "cannot tell".
  • An outcome mismatch never caps a grade. It decides where the link sits (section 6), and the grade is judged for the outcome measured.
  • Every departure from the table is stored as grade_override with a reason and appears in the per-link change log.

3.2.4 Adjudicating bodies

Rules T3, T4 and T9 treat a few bodies as adjudicators because they rule on causality for a named risk-outcome pair. The list is in section 10.2. Each body counts only for a pair it rated, which the field adjudication.pair_match records. Pooled cohort consortia (PSC, ERFC, CHANCES) size an association and issue no causal verdict. Each counts as one observational line.

3.2.5 Who decides: the script, the curator and the override

  • The script is binding for the rules that read structured fields (T1 to T4 and C1) when they fire at high confidence. The build lowers the grade to their ceiling unless the link carries a grade_override.
  • The rules that read text (T5 to T11 and C2 to C4) propose a grade. A curator confirms it.
  • A curator who departs from the table writes grade_override as {grade, reason}. The build audit fails any link whose grade differs from the table without an override. The override and its reason appear in the change log.
  • When two adjudicators rate the same pair more than one grade apart (secondhand smoke and coronary heart disease is the standing example: Burden of Proof gives two stars, and the US Surgeon General judged the link causal in 2006), the link becomes "cannot tell". The curator grades it and names both bodies in the reason.
  • A null trial or MR beside a clear association gives Suggestive by default. It gives Conflicting only when the trial or MR tested the same exposure on the same outcome and a curator confirms. The data record this as same_exposure and same_outcome on each trial or MR entry. A trial of a surrogate (exercise and falls, cognitive behavioural therapy, periodontal treatment and HbA1c) records same_outcome as false.
  • A high I-squared alone caps a single review at Suggestive. It often reflects spread in the size of an effect while every study points the same way.
  • The table never raises a grade. A Moderate link that meets every Strong condition waits for a curator review. There is no automatic upgrade.
  • A link marked "cannot tell" goes to a curator. The curator adds the evidence that the table names, where it exists, and records the grade and the reason in grade_basis. Where the evidence cannot be found, the grade that the verification pass judged most likely applies, and the basis says so.

3.3 Axis 2: effect magnitude and measure

Meta-relative-risk with 95% CI, always paired with an explicit exposure contrast and a contrast type:

  • binary: exposed against unexposed (current smoker against never smoker)
  • per-unit: per standardised increment (per 5 kg/m² BMI, per 20 mmHg SBP, per 50 g/day)
  • quantile: highest against lowest category (flagged, and not directly comparable to per-unit)
  • category: defined clinical strata

Each estimate also carries its measure: RR, HR, OR, SMR or rate ratio. The page shows the measure beside the estimate and gives each measure its own marker on the plot. For common outcomes an odds ratio overstates the risk ratio, and the page flags these links. The plotted ratio is shown as reported. Where a baseline exists, section 14 turns the ratio into people in 1,000, with a conversion that allows for this.

The contrast type and the measure are always shown. Lists sort by grade by default. A score sort is offered only inside one comparison type, with rows grouped by type.

3.4 Axis 3: the evidence strength score (ESS, 0 to 100)

The score is the composite the project was asked for, rebuilt to survive the objections of section 2.

ESS = 100 × M × R × C

M, the magnitude component (levels off, symmetric in direction):

M = |ln(RR)| / (|ln(RR)| + ln 2)

Half of the maximum is reached at RR = 2 (or 0.5). M keeps rising, so RR 20 still outranks RR 3, and a doubling of RR no longer doubles the score. Taking |ln(RR)| gives protective and harmful effects of equal size the same value. Direction is a separate field.

R, the replication component (diminishing returns):

R = 1 − exp(−N_eff / 2)

N_eff is the effective number of independent syntheses that the link cites, computed by the counting rule of section 5. It is never entered by hand. Under the rule N_eff runs from 1.00 to 2.31 in the current data, so R runs from 0.39 to 0.68:

C, the credibility multiplier, is the grade weight of section 3.1: Strong 1.00, Moderate 0.80, Suggestive 0.55, Weak 0.30, Conflicting 0.25.

Triangulation, heterogeneity, publication bias and reverse-causation risk are assessed when the grade is assigned. They are not applied again as separate multipliers, which would count them twice. Each link records the rules it met and the reason (grade_basis), so the grade can be audited.

How the score appears. Each panel shows the three parts, M, R and C, with one line of explanation, in a neutral colour. The score is stored with the link as ess, and its parts as score_parts. Links in the related-outcome layer carry no score (section 6).

4. What the score leaves out

These points are stated prominently on the site, because misreading the score is the most likely harm.

  1. Public health importance. The score measures how strong the evidence is for a cause-and-effect link. Public health importance is a separate question. A strong risk factor affecting 0.1% of people matters less to a population than a weak one affecting everybody. The population attributable fraction answers that question, and it depends on prevalence. Gestational diabetes and type 2 diabetes scores 34.9, for example, and gestational diabetes affects 5% to 9% of pregnancies in US data. Sedentary behaviour and type 2 diabetes scores 3.9, and many adults sit for more than seven hours a day. The second concerns far more people.
  2. Personal risk. The estimates are population-average relative risks. An individual's risk depends on baseline risk, dose, duration, age, genetics and competing risks.
  3. Comparison across contrast types. Per-unit, binary, quantile and category contrasts measure different things. Each estimate shows its type and its measure. Links of different comparison types are never ranked against each other by score.
  4. Related outcomes. A link whose estimate measures a wider group, a stand-in, a composite or the course of an existing disease carries no score (section 6).
  5. Currency. The score summarises a curated snapshot. The dataset is versioned and dated, and the explorer has no live update.

5. Counting replication: the review-counting rule

5.1 Why a rule

In versions before 2.0 the number of reviews behind each link was entered by hand, and no rule said which sources count or how overlap is handled. In 102 of 431 links the hand count was larger than the number of sources the link cites, so the reader could not see the reviews behind it. The hand-entered N_eff equalled the review count minus one, with a floor of 1, in 422 of 431 links, so it recorded no judgement about overlap. Version 2.0 computes both numbers from the sources a link cites.

5.2 The rule

The replication term uses only the sources a link cites. A cited source counts as a synthesis if it is a meta-analysis, an umbrella review, a pooled analysis of individual-participant data from a consortium, or an expert evidence review (GBD Burden of Proof, a Lancet Commission, a WHO review). Single trials, MR studies, cohorts, case-control studies, narrative reviews and methods papers do not count here. They shape the grade through the triangulation fields. A corrigendum never counts.

Syntheses overlap, because they pool many of the same studies. The count is discounted in three steps that need only recorded metadata:

  1. One programme or one review counts once. Syntheses from one programme (PSC, ERFC, IPD-Work, ESCAPE, CKD-PC, GBD with Burden of Proof, the Lancet dementia Commission, CTT, BPLTTC) and versions of one review form a single group. Syntheses by the same first author within five years also merge, once the source records carry first_author.
  2. An umbrella review counts once with the meta-analyses it covers. An umbrella review forms one group together with every meta-analysis on the same link from its year or earlier.
  3. Later groups count less. Order the remaining groups by year (groups from the same year by source id). The oldest counts 1. Each later group counts gap / (gap + 5), where gap is the number of years since the group before it. A group from the same year counts 0, one from 5 years later counts 0.5, and one from 10 years later counts 0.67.

N_eff is the sum of the weights, stored to two decimals. The panel lists every synthesis counted with its group and weight, so a reader can check any link by hand.

SYNTH = {meta, umbrella, pooled, synthesis}
H     = 5                      # years of gap at which a later synthesis counts one half

count(link):
    S = [s in link.sources where s.type in SYNTH
         and s.title does not start with Corrigendum, Erratum, Author Correction or Correction to
         and (s is not a synthesis of trials or MR studies, or s is link.estimate_source)]
    n_reviews = len(S)                       # never more than the number of cited sources

    if n_reviews == 0:
        return n_reviews = 0, n_eff = 1, flag "rests on primary studies"

    # step 1: same programme or same review -> one group
    merge(a, b) if family(a) == family(b) or normalised_title(a) == normalised_title(b)
    # once first_author is recorded:
    merge(a, b) if first_author(a) == first_author(b) and |a.year - b.year| <= 5

    # step 2: an umbrella review covers the meta-analyses before it
    merge(u, m) for umbrella u and meta-analysis m on the link with m.year <= u.year

    # step 3: year discount between groups
    groups = merged groups, sorted by (latest member year, first member id)
    n_eff  = 1                               # the oldest group counts in full
    for i = 1 .. len(groups) - 1:
        gap   = groups[i].year - groups[i-1].year
        n_eff = n_eff + gap / (gap + H)
    return n_reviews, round(n_eff, 2)

Three links of the dataset show the steps.

  • High blood pressure and coronary heart disease cites the Prospective Studies Collaboration analysis of 2002 and a pooled analysis of 2013 built on the same collaboration's data. They form one group (step 1). A meta-analysis of 2013 from another group comes in the same year, so it counts 0, and the Burden of Proof study of 2022 counts 9 / (9 + 5) = 0.64. N_eff = 1.64.
  • Sugar-sweetened drinks and type 2 diabetes cites an umbrella review of 2019 and a meta-analysis of 2015. The umbrella review covers it (step 2), so both form one group and N_eff = 1.00.
  • Processed meat and type 2 diabetes shows the year step (step 3), including a source from the same year as the one before it, which counts 0. Section 3.5 gives the sum.

5.3 Choices behind the rule

  • Expert evidence reviews count. GBD Burden of Proof, Lancet Commission and WHO reviews each run their own literature search. All papers from one programme count once.
  • Trials and MR stay in the grade. A trial or an MR study tests causality, and the grade already credits it. Counting it in replication as well would credit the same evidence twice. The exception is a trial or MR synthesis that carries the displayed estimate: CTT for statins with coronary heart disease and with ischaemic stroke, the blood-pressure-lowering trial synthesis for blood pressure treatment, the statin-trial meta-analysis for statins and type 2 diabetes, the influenza-vaccine trial synthesis, and the MR meta-analyses behind the four back pain links, smoking and adult asthma, and high uric acid and coronary heart disease. Its record is typed as a synthesis, and it counts once.
  • Links with no synthesis. Some links cite no synthesis. Their estimate rests on a trial, an MR study, a cohort or a case-control study. They receive N_eff 1 and a visible flag, "rests on primary studies". The flag keeps the score defined without implying that the evidence is absent.
  • The year step stands in for true overlap. A later review re-pools most of the studies of an earlier one and adds new ones in proportion to the years between them. The weight gap / (gap + 5) levels off in the same way as M. Changing 5 to 3 or 8 changes the results little.
  • The test for a second line counts groups. The year discount keeps two groups below N_eff 2, so rule T4 counts independent synthesis groups and other lines of evidence.

5.4 Limits of the rule

  • The umbrella step assumes an umbrella review covers every earlier meta-analysis on the same link. Some umbrella reviews cover a wider topic and may omit the cited meta-analysis. The assumption errs towards counting less.
  • The rule assumes every cited source is on topic for its link. No mechanical check can confirm this. A topic check is made by a curator whenever a source is attached.
  • Some pooled records are multi-cohort papers from one research group and sit close to a single primary study. Each counts as one synthesis.
  • In the current data N_eff runs from 1.00 to 2.31, so R runs from 0.39 to 0.68. R separates one synthesis from several, and the grade carries the rest of the evidence.
  • A source with no recorded year counts in full, and the build flags it.
  • A link counts the reviews it cites. A well-studied relationship that cites few reviews scores lower than the literature would suggest. The cure is to attach more verified reviews, each resolved against PubMed first, and each then counts for itself.

6. Related outcomes

6.1 The question

Each link records what its estimate measures: outcome_match and measured_outcome. The class answers one question: does the estimate measure the disease the link sits under?

A link leaves the counts and the score when its estimate measures:

  • a wider group that contains the disease (broader): cardiovascular disease under coronary heart disease, all stroke under ischaemic stroke, asthma at all ages under adult asthma;
  • a different measure that stands in for the disease (proxy): wheeze, a symptom score, a urine marker, bleeding;
  • a combined outcome that adds other events (composite_other): all-cause death, a blood marker, hypertension;
  • recurrence, worsening or complications in people who already have the disease, alone or pooled with new cases (progression).

A link whose estimate measures part of the disease (narrower: a subtype, one site, fatal cases only, a late stage) stays in the counts. Its panel title names the measured outcome. A link whose estimate measures the disease itself is exact.

6.2 Conventions

  • Acute or major coronary events count as coronary heart disease.
  • Fatal-only estimates are narrower.
  • Age, sex or population limits leave the class unchanged.
  • A prevalence design is graded by rule T5a and leaves the class unchanged.
  • Adult "diabetes" with no stated type counts as type 2.

7. Inclusion criteria and the position on Mendelian randomisation

Included:

  • Systematic reviews with meta-analysis of prospective observational studies (cohort, nested case-control), published in indexed journals
  • Meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials, and pooled trialist collaborations (CTT, BPLTTC)
  • Mendelian randomisation studies, including MR meta-analyses and large consortium MR
  • Pooled individual-participant-data consortia (for example the Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration and the Prospective Studies Collaboration)
  • Authoritative expert syntheses: GBD Burden of Proof, WCRF/AICR Continuous Update Project, IARC Monographs (for shared exposures), USPSTF, Lancet Commissions, WHO reviews

Excluded:

  • Narrative reviews, editorials and conference abstracts
  • Ecological studies

Kept with a ceiling. Some estimates rest on weaker designs, and the grade table caps them:

  • a single primary study (rule T5c) or a single trial, MR analysis or cohort, flagged "rests on primary studies";
  • meta-analyses restricted to cross-sectional or case-control designs (rules T5a and T5b).

The record of each link states the design behind the displayed estimate: estimate_design is one of cohort synthesis, pooled IPD, mixed, case-control, cross-sectional, trial synthesis, single trial, MR or single cohort, and estimate_source names the source.

Design decision. Restricting the dataset to systematic reviews is right for estimating an association and incomplete for establishing causation. A trial that overturns a literature must be admissible even though it is one study. The framework therefore admits landmark trials and MR as triangulation evidence. They are labelled as such, and they decide the grade through rules T2, T3 and C1 to C4.

Mendelian randomisation estimates. An MR estimate is an effect per unit of genetic liability. It is a different quantity from a person-level contrast. The framework treats it as follows.

  • Where a cited synthesis of prospective observational studies exists, the page displays that synthesis and keeps the MR result for triangulation. A cross-sectional synthesis does not qualify: high body-mass index and sleep apnoea displays the MR estimate because the only observational synthesis cited pools prevalence at a sleep study. One exception stands: high blood uric acid and gout displays the MR estimate of 2016 although an umbrella review of observational studies from 2017 is cited beside it and counts in the replication count. The owner may switch that link to the umbrella estimate.
  • Where no observational synthesis is cited, the page keeps the MR estimate and labels it "genetic estimate".
  • One MR analysis is one line of evidence, so rule T4 caps it at Moderate. One MR analysis in one cohort is capped at Suggestive by rule T5d.

8. Known limitations

  • Curated snapshot with no living-review update mechanism.
  • Effect estimates are extracted from published syntheses and inherit their biases.
  • Grade assignment combines a script and expert judgement. The rules that read text are proxies, and a curator confirms each change they propose. The inputs to every grade are recorded so that it can be contested.
  • Some fields that the rules would use are only partly filled. n_studies is recorded for 21 links and i2 for 6, read from the review each link displays; n_cases is recorded for none. Rules T9 and T10 read the field where it exists and the text of the caveat elsewhere. The fields stay empty in the remaining links until curation fills them.
  • Coverage is skewed towards well-studied risk factors and high-income-country populations. A missing link marks a gap in synthesised evidence and leaves the question of harm open.
  • Diseases and risk factors interact through mediation and confounding by shared causes. The graph shows marginal pairwise relationships and does not resolve causal pathways. A risk factor that acts entirely through another (adiposity, then blood pressure, then stroke) appears as a direct link to both. Mediation is noted per link where established and is not modelled.
  • The review count depends on the reviews a link cites. See section 5.4.

10. Provenance policy and the top-grade rules

10.1 Provenance

Part of the first dataset carried named, PubMed-verified citations and part did not. The pipeline records this for every link and displays it. Every link is tagged:

  • sourced: at least one named published source is attached, and that reference has been resolved against PubMed (title, journal, year, PMID, DOI). All 431 links of dataset v2.0 carry this tag.
  • editorial: the estimate reflects the curator's reading of the general literature, with no single citation attached. Such links are labelled "no named citation" everywhere they appear. No link carries this tag today. Version 1.0 listed 184 of 359 links in this class. Later audits sourced or removed them.

A link that is sourced and cites no synthesis carries the flag "rests on primary studies" (section 5.3).

10.2 The top grade: rules T3 and T4 and the adjudicating bodies

Version 1.0 enforced the top grade with one mechanical rule. Version 2.0 replaces it with two rules from the decision table. A link can hold Strong only if both hold.

  • T3, causal corroboration. A randomised or Mendelian-randomisation line supports the link, or an adjudicating body has rated this pair.
  • T4, more than one line. Two independent synthesis groups under the counting rule, or one group plus a second line for the same pair from another design (a prospective synthesis, a pooled consortium, a trial synthesis or MR). Case-control studies and sources for a broader outcome do not count as a second line. A trial estimate needs a second trial synthesis, an adjudicator or a supportive MR line for the same pair.

A link that fails either rule is capped at Moderate. This is deliberately conservative. Restoring a grade means attaching the missing citation or evidence.

  • The Prospective Studies Collaboration, the Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration and CHANCES are not adjudicators. They pool cohorts to size an association and issue no verdict on causality. Each counts as one observational line.
  • The pair match is checked for every body. The Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration reports no atrial fibrillation or kidney outcome, so it does not count for high blood pressure with atrial fibrillation or with chronic kidney disease.   • Burden of Proof ratings serve a second purpose. A 1-star rating sets Weak, a 2-star rating sets Suggestive and a 3-star rating sets Moderate (rule T11).

11. Citation verification

Fabricated references are the most damaging failure mode for a project like this. No reference is stored as a hand-typed identifier. scripts/verify_sources.py resolves each source against PubMed by title and records the returned PMID, DOI, journal and year.

The first pass exposed why this matters. Of 37 sources:

  • 7 failed to resolve at all and had to be found by targeted search;
  • 4 resolved to the wrong paper. A title-similarity match pointed "SPRINT" at a 2026 meta-analysis in Annals of Medicine, "Look AHEAD" at the U-TURN trial extension, "Steno-2" at a Brazilian cohort study, and "VITAL" at a JGIM commentary about VITAL;
  • 3 more resolved to a commentary, a correction notice and a foreign-language summary in place of the primary article: the Lancet dementia Commission, the secondhand-smoke Burden of Proof paper and the Cochrane salt meta-analysis.

Each of these would have been published as a plausible-looking but wrong citation. All 34 journal sources of that pass now resolve to the correct primary article. Three agency reports (WCRF, IARC, WHO) are not PubMed-indexed and carry direct URLs. The output is in validation/source_verification.json.

Later work extended the check.

  • The audit of 18 September 2026 re-checked every citation (519 at the time) and found no fabricated or mismatched identifier. It found a withdrawn Cochrane review as the only source for four links and a retracted paper on the reference page. Both were replaced in versions 1.3 and 1.4.
  • Every source attached after the first pass is resolved against PubMed before it is attached.
  • Every cited source has a record in data/dataset.json with its PMID, DOI or URL, and the reference page lists each one with a link to the paper.
  • Two further checks apply whenever a source is attached: the record gains first_author from PubMed, and a curator confirms that the paper is on topic for the link.

13. What has been checked and what has not

Method. The author ran the checks with AI-assisted verification. AI models compared each filed estimate with the paper it cites, applied the decision table in a second, independent implementation, and read each proposed grade change against its record. The author reviewed the results and decided every change. The change log lists each change with its reason.

Checked.

  • August 2026 literature audit: every estimate compared with the paper it cites, for the exact contrast stated.
  • September 2026 audit and its follow-up: citations re-checked, 100 flagged links re-checked against their sources, estimates corrected, unsupported links removed.
  • October 2026 review: contrasts rewritten in plain language, estimate types stated, exposure context added.
  • Grading reform of October 2026: the decision table was applied by two independent implementations, and a verification pass read each of the 97 proposed grade changes against its record. It accepted 62, rejected 23 and could not settle 12 from the record. A further 16 links that the first implementation left alone were also marked "cannot tell". The accepted changes are in the change log with their reasons.

Not checked.

  • No outside expert review of the effect estimates or the grades has been carried out.
  • The automated audit tests the dataset against its own rules. Matching each estimate to the literature is the AI-assisted work described above, and its errors can be shared with its sources.
  • Where a curation pass could not find the evidence that a "cannot tell" link needed, the grade rests on the best estimate of the verification pass, and the grade basis says so.
  • The fields n_studies and i2 are filled for 21 and 6 links. Elsewhere they are empty, so rules T9 and T10 rest partly on caveat text.
  • Moderate links that meet every Strong condition have not been reviewed for an upgrade.
  • Link-level texts (comparison, caveat and notes) are written in English and Chinese. The other languages show the English text until they are translated.

14. In 1,000 people: the absolute-risk illustration

14.1 Why the page shows people

A relative effect compares two groups. It does not say how many people are affected: doubling a rare risk adds few cases, and a small rise in a common risk adds many. Until version 2.0 the page said so and stopped there. Reporting and communication guidance asks for more. STROBE item 16(c) asks authors to consider translating relative into absolute risk for a meaningful period. NICE guideline NG197 (recommendations 1.4.5 to 1.4.11) asks for absolute risk, one denominator, a defined period, both the affected and the unaffected, and a picture with the numbers. Systematic reviews find that relative formats make an effect look larger and more persuasive than the same effect in absolute terms (Akl 2011; Zipkin 2014).

From version 2.1 a link shows, beside its relative effect, how many of 1,000 people in the comparison group have or develop the disease and how many in the group with the risk factor would. The page calls this the people count. It is the default view of every panel; the relative effect and its plot are one click away.

14.2 What a reader sees

  • The difference first. "About 150 more in 1,000". Readers get absolute differences wrong more often when they must subtract two frequencies themselves (Woloshin and Schwartz 2011), so the page does the subtraction.
  • Both counts, with their time frame. "Without this factor, about 260 in 1,000 people develop it over a lifetime. With it, and living as long, about 410 would." The time frame is over a lifetime, by a stated age, or at any one time, as the baseline defines it. Lifetime figures look large beside ten-year figures (Navar 2018), and the page says so.
  • One denominator. 1,000 on every link, in words and in the picture. Changing denominators and "1 in X" formats mislead (Sirota 2014).
  • A picture of all 1,000. Ten blocks of 100 dots: the comparison group's count, then the additional people in a second colour, then the unaffected. For a factor that lowers the risk, the people spared are outlined. Icon arrays help comparison and counter the neglect of the denominator (Garcia-Retamero and Cokely 2017); highlighting the additional cases inside the full array lowers undue worry (Zikmund-Fisher 2008). In a panel, each row carries a bar on a shared scale, because bars read more accurately than large arrays for counts above about 100 (McCaffery 2012).
  • The range once, in words. "The studies fit 120 to 180 more." Ranges cost comprehension (Sladakovic 2016) and little trust (van der Bles 2020), so the range is given once and kept out of the picture.
  • No size words. The page gives no label such as "large" or "small". Verbal labels inflate estimates (Büchter 2014).
  • Wording tied to the grade. A Strong link states the counts. A Moderate link adds "This factor is probably a cause. The counts hold if it is." A Suggestive link adds "It is not settled that this factor is a cause. The counts hold only if it is." This follows the informative statements of GRADE guidance 26 (Santesso 2020). Weak and Conflicting links get no count.
  • Counts for groups. The counts describe groups of people and are never worded as the reader's own risk. The note under each count says that it cannot tell who will be affected (Rose 1985; Freeman 2021).

Two findings argue against parts of this design, and the page keeps to its choice knowingly. Frequencies were understood no better than percentages in two large trials, and "x in 1,000" felt riskier than the same percentage (Woloshin and Schwartz 2011; Freeman 2021). The page uses frequencies because NICE recommends them and because whole people need no decimal point for small risks. The display has not been tested with lay readers (section 14.7).

14.3 The calculation

The principle is that of GRADE "Summary of findings" tables: an assumed risk in the comparison group and a relative effect give the corresponding risk in the other group (Cochrane Handbook, sections 14.1.5 and 15.4.4; GRADE guidance 12 and 27). The code is scripts/absolute_risk.py; it runs at every build and has its own tests. With P the baseline as a proportion and R the relative effect:

  1. One conversion for every ratio. The odds of the disease are multiplied by R: r1 = R × r0 / (1 − r0 + R × r0). For an odds ratio this is the Cochrane formula. For a hazard ratio, a rate ratio or a relative risk pooled from cohorts, Cochrane gives 1 − (1 − r0)^R, which holds when nobody dies of anything else. Over a lifetime death competes with the disease, and where the disease rate and the death rate rise with age in step, the lifetime odds are the ratio of the two rates, so a hazard ratio multiplies the odds. A life table with a Gompertz death rate (review-2026-11-11/scripts/lifetime_link_check.py in the site repository) confirmed this: the odds form was exact where the two rates rose in step. Elsewhere it was within 30 per 1,000 for relative effects up to 2 and within 54 for effects up to 3.5, while the Cochrane form was off by up to 95 and up to 178 for the same inputs. The odds form never exceeds 1 and is always closer to no difference than r0 × R.
  2. The comparison group. A population's risk is the average of its groups' risks. The baseline P describes the whole population, which includes people with the risk factor, so the comparison group's risk r0 is solved so that the groups, weighted by their shares, give back P. This is the logic of absolute-risk models since Gail and colleagues (1989), applied to a cumulative risk.
    • Two groups (current smokers against never smokers; exposed_vs_unexposed in the data): the share of adults in the first-named group comes from a published source. Where the comparison leaves out a group that is also at raised risk (former smokers, people who are overweight when obese are compared with normal weight), that group enters with its own share and its own relative effect from this dataset, or midway on the multiplicative scale when the dataset has none. Where two links of one disease share a comparison group (current smokers and former smokers, each against never smokers), both use the same shares, so that the comparison group has one count on the page.
    • Highest against lowest group of a ranked exposure (high_vs_low): the population is taken as three equal groups with effects 1, √R and R. The comparison group is the one with effect 1, also where R is below 1.
    • Per step and two levels (per_step, level_vs_level): the comparison is a person at the usual level, r0 = P. This is used only for a relative effect of at most 1.5, because for larger effects the average risk of a population is well above the risk at the average level.
  3. The range repeats steps 1 and 2 at both ends of the interval of R. It covers the relative effect only.
  4. Rounding. Whole people per 1,000, and tens from 100 upwards; a half rounds up. The comparison group's count, the difference and both ends of the range are each rounded on their own. The second count is the first plus the difference, so the three numbers add up.

14.4 The inputs

All inputs are in data/absolute_risk.json, each figure with its source, and on the page People counts: baselines and sources.

  • Baselines. One per disease: how common the disease is in a named general population. A lifetime risk from a population-based cohort or register where one was published, otherwise a prevalence in adults. The case definition has to match the tool's disease. A link for men or for women uses that sex's figure; where a source gives figures for men and women only, a link for both sexes uses their mean. Where published figures differ several-fold (Parkinson's disease, cirrhosis) or describe another group than the links (asthma, eczema), the disease has no baseline. Baselines differ between populations, sometimes twofold; the companion page gives the range for each disease.
  • Shares. For a two-group comparison, the share of adults in the first-named group, from WHO, NCD-RisC, the Global Burden of Disease study or a national survey.
  • Whether a count fits. Two readers judged independently, for each of the 286 links graded Strong, Moderate or Suggestive with an interval that excludes no difference, whether a long-term count for the general population is a fair statement. They agreed on 278. The eight others were settled on the cautious side, apart from two very large effects that the build withholds for that reason.

Every figure in use was read in its source twice: by the researcher who found it and by a second reader who saw only the figure and the citation. Figures the second reading could not confirm were dropped or replaced, and three were confirmed in the report text by a third reading (review-2026-11-11/verify/ in the site repository).

14.6 What the counts assume

  1. They are an illustration. A relative effect from one set of studies is applied to a baseline from another source and another population.
  2. They hold only if the link is causal and the published effect is free of confounding. The grade says how far that is established, and the wording follows the grade.
  3. The relative effect is taken as constant across age, sex and country. Ratio measures are more stable across baseline risks than risk differences in most empirical comparisons (Deeks 2002; Furukawa 2002), with dissent (Poole 2015; Doi 2022). Effects estimated over ten or twenty years of follow-up are carried over a lifetime.
  4. Both groups are taken to live equally long. Many risk factors also shorten life, and then fewer people live to develop a disease of old age. Measured lifetime risks show this. In the ARIC cohort, people with diabetes diagnosed before age 60 had more dementia by age 80 than people without diabetes (161 against 94 per 1,000) and a lower lifetime risk (360 against 456), because they died earlier (Hu 2024). The count on this page, 220 against 330, describes people who live as long. The note under each lifetime count says that the real count is lower where a factor also shortens life.
  5. Where a cohort measured the exposed group's risk, the counts are of the same size. People with obesity and venous thromboembolism: 109 per 1,000 on this page, 109 measured in ARIC from age 45 (Bell 2016). Current smokers and abdominal aortic aneurysm: 142 on this page, 105 measured in ARIC from age 45 to 85 (Tang 2016). Both baselines come from the same two papers, so these comparisons test the calculation and the shares, and leave the baselines untested.
  6. The baseline is one population. Another population, period or case definition moves both counts. The range under a count does not cover this.
  7. A group average says nothing about one person. Age, sex, family history, dose and duration all matter.

14.7 What has been checked and what has not

Checked: the calculation has automated tests (the groups' weighted risks give back the baseline; rounding; ranges ordered); the build stops on an input without a source; every baseline and share in use was confirmed by a second reading of its source; the eligibility of every link was judged twice; the conversion was tested against a life table; two counts were compared with risks measured in cohorts (section 14.6).

Not checked: the display has not been tested with lay readers, which the standards for patient decision aids ask for (Zikmund-Fisher 2025). No outside expert has reviewed the baselines or the choice of populations. Shares of adults exposed mostly describe the world or one country, and baselines another population. The square-root rule for a group without its own estimate and the three-equal-groups rule are modelling conventions; no methods paper was found that names either.

The framework and the dataset share a version number from 2.0 onwards. Link-level changes are in the change log.

1.224 August 2026First published page: 456 links.
1.39 September 2026Evidence audit: group classifications fixed, unsupported links dropped, H. pylori split from its eradication therapy. 442 links. Clean-ups followed on 18 September.

Three diseases are renamed (Depression, Back pain, Fracture in adults). The change log of every link is published beside this page.
2.112 November 2026 (built)"In 1,000 people": 179 links show how many of 1,000 people in the comparison group have or develop the disease and how many in the group with the risk factor would (section 14). A baseline for 34 diseases, with sources. No link changed its estimate or its grade.

What changed for readers of version 1.0

  • Grade names. Convincing, Probable, Limited and Contested became Strong, Moderate, Weak and Conflicting. Each grade has a one-line definition for readers (section 3.1).
  • Grade assignment. The decision table (section 3.2) replaces expert judgement. Every grade that departs from the table carries a reason.
  • The top grade. The single mechanical rule of version 1.0 became rules T3 and T4 (section 10.2). The Prospective Studies Collaboration, the Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration and CHANCES are no longer adjudicators, and the list of adjudicating bodies is checked pair by pair.
  • Counting. The review count and N_eff are computed from the sources a link cites (section 5). The worked examples of section 3.5 use the new N_eff and links of this dataset.
  • Outcomes. Links whose estimate measures something other than the named disease sit in a related-outcome layer outside the counts and the score (section 6).
  • Estimates. The estimate may come from a trial synthesis or a Mendelian randomisation analysis, with a ceiling on the grade (section 7). Each estimate shows its measure (section 3.3).
  • The score. The panel shows its three parts. Lists sort by grade, and the score sorts only inside one comparison type (section 3.3).
